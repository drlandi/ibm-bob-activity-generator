🎣 Narrative Hook
It is August 1947. Your grandmother is standing in the doorway of the only home she has ever known. Outside, the street she grew up on is suddenly on the wrong side of a new border. She has one hour to decide: pack what she can carry and leave forever, or stay and hope for the best. What does she take? What does she leave behind? What does she become in that moment?

📋 Step-by-Step Instructions
Before class:
Pre-arrange desks in clusters of 3–4. Print and distribute materials into labeled folders at each cluster. Review trauma-informed facilitation guidelines and establish that this topic involves real human suffering — set behavioral expectations in advance.
Emotional Check-In (5 min): Open by reading the Narrative Hook aloud. Ask students to silently rate their emotional readiness to engage (thumbs up / sideways / down — no explanation required). Acknowledge the weight of the topic. Remind students of classroom community agreements.
Mini-Lecture: Setting the Scene (10 min): Using the board timeline poster, briefly cover: the announcement of Partition (June 1947), the drawing of the Radcliffe Line, the human scale of displacement (~14 million people), and the communal violence that accompanied the migration. Keep language precise — this was a documented humanitarian catastrophe. Invite no debate on political legitimacy at this stage; frame it as historical fact.
Primary Source Reading (10 min): Distribute testimony excerpt sheets. Students read silently, then one volunteer reads aloud per excerpt. After each, pause for 10 seconds of silence before moving on — this models respect for survivor voice. Prompt students to underline one phrase that stays with them.
Distribution of Family Scenario Cards (2 min): Each student or pair receives a card describing a fictional composite family (e.g., "The Khans live in Lahore. He is a schoolteacher. She is pregnant. They have a Hindu neighbor who has been their friend for twenty years. It is August 13, 1947."). Emphasize these are composite characters — not real people — designed to humanize the historical scale.
Decision Point Roleplay (15 min): Using the Decision Prompt Sheet, students work individually or in pairs to respond in writing to four sequential decision moments:
- Do you flee immediately, wait, or send family ahead?
- What three objects do you take? What do you leave?
- A neighbor from a different religion asks for help. What do you do?
- Weeks later, you have survived. What is the first thing you feel?
Students write their answers as if they are the family, in first person. Remind them there are no "correct" answers — this is about empathy and historical imagination.
Small Group Discussion (10 min): Groups of 3–4 share their decisions. Teacher circulates and listens. Prompt: "Did different families in your group make different choices? Why? What factors made the difference?" Discourage students from judging the choices as right or wrong.
Individual Reflection Journaling (10 min): Students write independently in response to one of the Reflection Questions (below). This is private unless students choose to share.
Full-Class Debrief (8 min): Bring the class together. Ask: "What patterns did you notice across groups? What does this exercise tell us about how ordinary people experience historical events?" Transition from the personal to the structural: name that millions of real families lived through exactly this. Connect to Social Studies themes of migration, borders, identity, and belonging.
Exit Ticket (2 min): Students complete a printed slip answering: "Name one thing you learned today that you did not know before" and "Name one question you are still sitting with." Collect these — they serve as both formative assessment and emotional temperature check.

💬 Reflection Questions
Empathy & Agency: In your scenario, what was the hardest decision your family had to make — and what made it hard? What would you have done, and does that question feel fair to even ask?
Scale & Humanity: Historians estimate 14–20 million people were displaced and up to 2 million died during Partition. How does your family's fictional story change how you hold that number?
Identity & Borders: The border was drawn in a matter of weeks by a British lawyer who had never visited India. What does it mean for an external authority to draw a line through the middle of someone's life?
Ordinary People in History: How is studying the choices of ordinary families different from studying the decisions of political leaders? What does each kind of history teach us?
Connection to Today: Where in the world today are families making similar choices — to flee or stay? What are the similarities and differences to 1947?
