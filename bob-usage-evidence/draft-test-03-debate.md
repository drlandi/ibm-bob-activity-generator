🎣 Narrative Hook
It's 2031, and your city must choose: renew its contract with the coal plant that's powered the region for 60 years, or invest in a new solar grid that costs twice as much upfront. Jobs, electricity bills, and the local environment all hang in the balance. Today, you're not just a student — you're a policy advisor, and the council is listening.

📋 Step-by-Step Instructions
Open with the Narrative Hook (5 min). Read the city scenario aloud or project it. Ask: "What would you recommend, and what would you need to know first?" Accept 2–3 quick responses without judgment.
Frame the debate motion (2 min). Write on the board: "Resolved: Government funding should prioritize renewable energy over fossil fuels." Clarify that students will argue an assigned position — not necessarily their personal view.
Assign teams and roles (3 min). Divide the class into:
- Team A – Affirmative (Pro-Renewable): argues for the resolution
- Team B – Negative (Pro-Fossil Fuel): argues against the resolution
- Panel Judges (2–3 students): score arguments using the rubric
- Fact-Checkers (1–2 students): flag any unsupported statistical claims
Distribute the approved sources list and argument planner 📝 [INSERT STUDENT WORKSHEET HERE]. Explain the Claim → Evidence → Warrant structure students must use for every argument point.
Computer lab research phase (20 min). Students research independently or in pairs. Require each team to collect at least 4 cited statistics from credible sources (year, author/organization, URL). Fact-Checkers may also research during this time.
Team prep and argument construction (10 min). Teams huddle to organize their 3 strongest points into an opening statement and anticipate 2 likely counterarguments for the rebuttal round.
Opening statements (6 min). Each team delivers a 2–3 minute opening. Judges take notes; Fact-Checkers log every statistic cited.
Rebuttal round (5 min). Each team has 1.5 minutes to directly respond to the opposing team's opening. Teams must address specific claims made — not introduce entirely new evidence.
Cross-examination (8 min). Judges and Fact-Checkers may ask 2–3 pointed questions to each team. Examples:
- "You cited a 2019 IEA figure — has that projection been updated?"
- "How does your proposal address energy reliability during peak demand?"
Closing statements (5 min). Each team delivers a 1.5-minute summary. No new evidence; synthesis only.
Class vote and reveal (4 min). Audience votes on which team argued most effectively (not which position they personally believe). Judges announce scores and explain one strength and one gap per team.
Full-class debrief (2 min). Ask: "Did anyone argue a position they personally disagree with? What was that like?" Briefly surface the distinction between advocacy skill and personal belief.
Reflection journaling (10 min). Students respond to reflection questions independently in writing.

💬 Reflection Questions
Evidence quality: Which statistic you encountered during research surprised you the most, and why? Did it change how you thought about the topic?
Perspective-taking: If you argued a position you personally disagree with, what did it feel like to defend it? What did you learn from that experience?
Economic vs. environmental trade-offs: Both sides raised real costs and benefits. In your own view, how should policymakers weigh short-term economic impact against long-term environmental outcomes?
Source credibility: What made some statistics more persuasive than others during the debate? How did you evaluate whether a source was trustworthy?
Policy complexity: Is "prioritize one over the other" even the right question for governments to ask? What alternative policy framings might be more accurate or useful?

🎨 Customization Zones
Adapt the resolution to local context ✏️ [INSERT TEACHER NOTE HERE] — Swap the generic resolution for a real pending bill, a local utility decision, or a regional energy story in your area. Students research a live issue, raising stakes and authenticity.
Differentiate the research scaffolding 📝 [INSERT STUDENT WORKSHEET HERE] — For struggling learners, pre-populate the worksheet with 2 starter sources and partially completed claim frames. For advanced students, remove the approved sources list and require independent source evaluation.
Add a visual anchor to the hook 📷 [INSERT PHOTO OR IMAGE HERE] — Open with a satellite image comparing a coal region to a solar farm region, or a chart showing your country's current energy mix. This grounds the abstract policy debate in visible, data-driven reality.
Expand the Fact-Checker role ✏️ [INSERT TEACHER NOTE HERE] — In classes with strong media literacy foundations, have Fact-Checkers submit a short written report after the debate identifying every unverified or misleading statistic used, turning the role into a standalone assessment artifact.
