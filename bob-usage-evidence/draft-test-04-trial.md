🎣 Narrative Hook
A student at Jefferson High is accused of stealing a rival school's mascot costume the night before the big game. The evidence? A witness who claims they saw someone matching the accused's description, a receipt for supplies found in a locker, and a very nervous alibi. Today, the classroom becomes Courtroom 101 — and justice is in your hands.

📋 Step-by-Step Instructions
Before Class
Prepare and print the case packet (see Customization Zones). The fictional case should include: a one-paragraph case summary, a list of three charges, four witness statements (two favorable to each side), and a short list of evidence items.
Arrange desks into a courtroom layout: two counsel tables facing a teacher desk (the bench), a single chair to the side as the witness stand, and a cluster of desks for the jury.
Write the charges on the board and post a brief glossary of terms: plaintiff, defendant, objection, sustained, overruled, direct examination, cross-examination, verdict.
Opening (10 min)
Distribute role cards and case packets as students enter. Assign roles by drawing cards or using a pre-made list. Groups: Prosecution team (2 attorneys), Defense team (2 attorneys), Witnesses (4–6 students with individual character sheets), Jury (6–8 students), Judge (1 student), Bailiff (1 student — calls order, swears in witnesses).
Give students 5 minutes to read their materials silently and 3 minutes to huddle with their team. Jurors read the case summary only — they must not see witness prep notes.
The Judge calls the court to order. Bailiff reads the charge aloud.
Opening Statements (8 min)
Prosecution delivers a 2-minute opening statement arguing why the defendant is guilty. Attorneys may use notes.
Defense delivers a 2-minute opening statement arguing for innocence. Remind students: opening statements are previews, not arguments — they tell the jury what the evidence will show.
Judge maintains time; use the gavel to signal when to stop.
Direct Examinations (20 min)
Prosecution calls its witnesses one at a time. Each direct examination lasts ~2–3 minutes. Prosecution attorneys ask open-ended questions; witnesses answer using their character sheet, but may add reasonable details.
After each prosecution witness, Defense may cross-examine for up to 2 minutes using leading questions to challenge credibility or introduce doubt.
Defense then calls its witnesses following the same format, with Prosecution cross-examining after each.
Closing Arguments (8 min)
Prosecution delivers a 3-minute closing argument summarizing evidence and urging a guilty verdict.
Defense delivers a 3-minute closing argument emphasizing reasonable doubt and weaknesses in the prosecution's case.
Remind students: closing arguments are persuasive — this is where Language Arts skills shine. Encourage rhetorical devices: repetition, emotional appeal, logical structure.
Deliberation & Verdict (11 min)
Jurors move their desks together. Each juror writes their initial vote (Guilty / Not Guilty) on an index card. The Judge presides over deliberation but does not vote.
Jurors discuss for 5 minutes, citing specific evidence from the trial (not outside knowledge). After deliberation, conduct a final vote.
The foreperson (jury elects one) announces the verdict to the court. The Judge reads the verdict formally.
Debrief (5 min)
Step out of character. Ask the class: Was the verdict fair? Was any evidence ignored? Did the best argument win?
Briefly discuss how real trials differ: professional attorneys, rules of evidence, longer timelines, appeals.
---
💬 Reflection Questions
Role perspective: What was the hardest part of arguing your assigned side, especially if you personally believed the opposite?
Persuasion analysis: Which opening or closing statement was most persuasive? What specific language or rhetorical techniques made it effective?
Reasonable doubt: The standard in criminal trials is beyond reasonable doubt — not probably guilty. Did the evidence in this case meet that standard? Why or why not?
Fairness and procedure: What courtroom rules (objections, structured speaking turns) existed to make the process fair? What could go wrong without them?
Real-world connection: How does the right to a trial by jury protect people in a democracy? Can you think of a historical case where this protection mattered?

Argument Construction
Claims are vague or unsupported by case evidence
Claims are clear and supported by at least one piece of evidence
Claims are clear, well-structured, and supported by multiple pieces of evidence with logical reasoning
Courtroom Participation
Student reads directly from notes with minimal engagement; rarely responds to opposing side
Student engages with the case and responds to some opposing arguments
Student actively listens, responds directly to opposing points, and adapts their argument dynamically
Use of Persuasive Language
Language is informal or list-like; no rhetorical devices used
Some persuasive language present (e.g., clear thesis, basic appeal to logic)
Effective use of rhetorical devices (repetition, ethos/pathos/logos); language is compelling and audience-aware
Civic Knowledge
Misuses or ignores legal vocabulary and courtroom procedures
Uses most legal terms correctly; follows basic procedure
Accurately applies legal vocabulary throughout; demonstrates understanding of due process and burden of proof
