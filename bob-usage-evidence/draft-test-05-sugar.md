🎣 Narrative Hook
The school cafeteria is buzzing with a mystery: the kitchen staff noticed that sugar seems to disappear into hot tea almost instantly, but takes forever to dissolve in cold milk. A student asks, "Does it matter what liquid you use, or just how hot it is?" Your team of scientists has been called in to design the definitive experiment — but first, you need to make sure the test is fair.

🧰 Materials
Granulated white sugar (standardized amount — e.g., 1 teaspoon per trial)
Three liquids: water, whole milk, white vinegar
Ice (for cold temperature trials)
Hot water source or kettle (for warm/hot temperature trials)
Thermometer (1 per group)
Clear plastic cups or beakers (9 per group — 3 liquids × 3 temperatures)
Stirring rods or spoons (designated per cup to avoid cross-contamination)
Stopwatch or timer (phone is fine)
Measuring spoons (teaspoon)
Ruler
Graph paper or computer with spreadsheet software (optional)
Student lab planning/recording worksheet
Safety goggles

📋 Step-by-Step Instructions
Before the lab:
Set the scene with the Narrative Hook. Ask: "What do you predict will happen? Does the liquid type matter? Does temperature?" Take a quick show-of-hands poll to surface prior knowledge.
Introduce the scientific method framing. Write on the board:
- Independent variables: type of liquid, temperature of liquid
- Dependent variable: time for sugar to dissolve (seconds)
- Controlled variables (everything kept the same): amount of sugar, amount of liquid, container size, whether/how much you stir
Discuss "fair testing." Ask students: "If we stir one cup but not another, is that fair? Why not?" Decide as a class on a stirring protocol (e.g., 10 stirs at a steady pace immediately after adding sugar) and record it on the board as a class norm.
Have students write a hypothesis in the form: "I predict that \_\_\_ liquid at \_\_\_ temperature will dissolve sugar fastest because \_\_\_."
Distribute materials. Each group receives 9 cups. Label them with masking tape:
- Water Cold / Water Room Temp / Water Hot
- Milk Cold / Milk Room Temp / Milk Hot
- Vinegar Cold / Vinegar Room Temp / Vinegar Hot
Prepare temperature conditions:
- Cold: liquid poured over a small amount of ice, equilibrate 2 minutes, remove ice before trial, measure temperature (~4–8 °C)
- Room temperature: liquid straight from container (~20–22 °C)
- Hot: warm water from kettle, or microwaved (~50–55 °C — always use goggles; teacher supervises)
Measure and add liquid — 150 mL into each cup. Confirm temperature with thermometer and record it on the data table.
Run trials: One student adds exactly 1 tsp of sugar, a second student starts the timer, a third stirs (using the agreed protocol). Stop the timer when no visible sugar crystals remain at the bottom. Record the time in seconds. If sugar does not fully dissolve within 3 minutes, record ">180 s."
Repeat for all 9 conditions. Encourage students to note observations beyond just time (color change? residue? smell?).
Record data in the class data table (aggregate results from all groups on the board or shared spreadsheet for a richer dataset).
Math Integration — Graphing:
Students create a grouped bar chart with:
- X-axis: Liquid type (Water, Milk, Vinegar)
- Y-axis: Time to dissolve (seconds)
- Three bars per liquid (cold, room temp, hot) in different colors
- Title, axis labels, and a legend (reinforce graph conventions)
Optional (computer): Enter group data into a spreadsheet; use a chart wizard to auto-generate the bar chart. Compare computer graph to hand-drawn graph for accuracy.
Debrief:
Return to the hypothesis. Did results match predictions? What surprised the group?
Address the "fair test" question explicitly: Ask students to identify one moment in the lab where it was hard to keep things controlled. What would they do differently?
---
💬 Reflection Questions
Which variable — liquid type or temperature — appeared to have a greater effect on how quickly sugar dissolved? What in your data supports that conclusion?
Was your test truly "fair"? Identify one variable that was difficult to control perfectly and explain how it might have affected your results.
Your data shows sugar dissolved more slowly in milk than in water at the same temperature. What properties of milk (think: fats, proteins) might explain this? How could you design a follow-up experiment to test your idea?
Vinegar is acidic and water is neutral — does the acidity of a liquid affect how quickly sugar dissolves? Is this something your experiment could answer, or would you need a different design? Why?
In real life, scientists repeat experiments many times. How did combining your class's results change (or confirm) what your group found alone? Why is replication important?
