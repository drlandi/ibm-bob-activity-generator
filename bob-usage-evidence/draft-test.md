🎣 Narrative Hook
A skydiver jumps from a plane and, before opening her parachute, notices every second she falls faster and faster — but by exactly the same amount each second. What invisible force is pulling her downward with such perfect consistency? Today, you'll step into her shoes (safely!) and measure that force yourself using nothing but a ball, a meter stick, and a stopwatch.

🧰 Materials
Tennis ball or rubber ball (1 per group)
Measuring tape or meter stick
Stopwatch or smartphone timer (1 per group)
Masking tape (for marking drop heights on the wall)
Calculator
Lab worksheet (one per student)
Graph paper or graphing tool (optional)
Safety goggles (recommended)
---

📋 Step-by-Step Instructions
Open with the hook. Read or paraphrase the skydiver scenario aloud. Ask students: "What do you think causes her to speed up? Does she speed up the same way every time?" Take 2–3 responses.
Activate prior knowledge. Write g = ? on the board and ask if anyone knows the value. Record guesses without confirming yet.
Safety briefing. Remind students to drop (not throw) the ball vertically and to keep the area below the drop zone clear.
Divide into groups of 3–4 students. Assign roles: Dropper, Timer, Recorder, and Calculator.
Mark drop heights on the wall using masking tape at 0.5 m, 1.0 m, 1.5 m, and 2.0 m above the floor (measured with the meter stick).
Conduct trials. For each height, the Dropper holds the ball at the marked height and releases it (no push). The Timer starts the stopwatch at release and stops it at impact. Perform 3 trials per height and record all times on the worksheet.
Average the times. Students calculate the average time for each drop height using their 3 trial values.
Apply the kinematic equation. Using d = ½ × g × t², rearrange to solve for g:
> g = 2d / t²

Students calculate g for each drop height and record results.

Compare results. Each group writes their calculated g values on the board. As a class, calculate the class average and compare it to the accepted value of 9.8 m/s².
Analyze error. Lead a discussion: Why might your value differ from 9.8? Guide students toward concepts like reaction time, air resistance, and measurement precision.
Wrap up. Return to the board and confirm the accepted value. Ask: "What would happen to a falling object if there were no gravity? What if gravity were twice as strong?"
---

💬 Reflection Questions
How close was your group's calculated value of g to 9.8 m/s²? What do you think caused any difference?
Why did we drop the ball from multiple heights instead of just one? How did that improve the experiment?
If you repeated this experiment on the Moon (where g ≈ 1.6 m/s²), how would the fall times change?
What does it mean to say gravity is an acceleration rather than just a force? How did your data show that?
How might this experiment be redesigned to get a more accurate measurement of g?

📊 Assessment Rubric
Criterion	Proficient (3)	Developing (2)	Beginning (1)
Data Collection	All trials recorded accurately with correct units and consistent method	Most trials recorded; minor gaps or unit errors	Incomplete data; inconsistent or missing trials
Mathematical Accuracy	g = 2d/t² applied correctly for all heights; average computed	Formula applied with minor arithmetic errors	Formula misapplied or calculation absent
Error Analysis	Clearly identifies 2+ sources of error and explains their effect on results	Identifies 1 source of error with partial explanation	Vague or no error analysis
Reflection Quality	Responses use physics vocabulary and connect evidence to concepts	Responses show partial understanding; limited evidence use	Responses are superficial or off-topic
