# Statistics: Mean vs. Average — Distinguishing Measures of Central Tendency

## Premise Evaluation

Evaluating the premise: The topic "difference between mean and average" needs careful evaluation. In common usage, "average" most often refers to the mean, but statistically, "average" is an umbrella term covering mean, median, and mode. The premise that they are different is partially true (mean is one specific type of average) but can be misleading if framed as "mean ≠ average." This is not a false premise — it's a genuine and important statistical nuance worth teaching correctly. The activity will teach the accurate distinction: mean is a type of average; average is not a single measure.

---

## Narrative Hook

A viral social media post claims: "The average salary at BoostCorp is $85,000 — so employees are doing great!" But when a job-seeker joins and checks the payroll spreadsheet, something feels off — most of her colleagues earn around $42,000. One CEO salary of $1.2 million is quietly pulling the "average" way up. Who's telling the truth, and what does "average" actually mean?

## Step-by-Step Instructions

1. **Open with the hook (8 min).** Display the social media post scenario on the projector. Ask students: "Does this claim sound fair? What would you want to know before believing it?" Take 3–4 responses without correcting anyone yet.

2. **Clarify the terminology (10 min).** Explain the core concept directly:
   - "Average" is a colloquial umbrella term — it can refer to mean, median, or mode depending on context.
   - "Mean" is one specific mathematical operation: sum of all values ÷ number of values.
   - In everyday speech, people say "average" when they almost always mean arithmetic mean — and that conflation causes real-world misunderstandings.
   - Write the three measures of central tendency on the board: Mean, Median, Mode, and briefly define each.
   - ✏️ [INSERT TEACHER NOTE HERE] — Emphasize that neither "mean" nor "average" is wrong; the issue is precision and context. In business reports, always ask which measure is being used.

3. **Set up the spreadsheet (7 min).** Students open the worksheet file or manually enter the provided salary dataset into column A (rows 1–20). Label column A "Salary". The dataset should include approximately 18 salaries in the $38,000–$65,000 range and one outlier (e.g., CEO salary: $1,200,000).

4. **Calculate all three measures (12 min).** Students use spreadsheet formulas to compute:
   - Mean: =AVERAGE(A1:A20)
   - Median: =MEDIAN(A1:A20)
   - Mode: =MODE(A1:A20)
   
   Students record all three values in a summary table on their worksheet.
   - 📷 [INSERT PHOTO OR IMAGE HERE] — Screenshot or photo of a completed sample spreadsheet showing all three formulas and results side by side.

5. **Manipulate the outlier (8 min).** Students change the CEO salary cell to different values (e.g., $80,000 / $500,000 / $5,000,000) and observe how each measure responds. They record how much the mean shifts versus how little the median shifts.

6. **Business interpretation discussion (8 min).** In pairs or trios, students discuss:
   - "If you were a job applicant, which measure would you want a company to report, and why?"
   - "If you were the CEO writing a press release, which measure might you prefer to highlight?"
   - "Is reporting the mean salary misleading, or just incomplete?"

7. **Group share-out (5 min).** Each group shares one insight. Teacher synthesizes on the board: connect student observations to the concept that word choice in data reporting carries ethical weight — a core idea in both statistics and business communications.

8. **Reflection journaling (7 min).** Students respond individually to the reflection questions (see below).

---

## Reflection Questions

1. In your own words, explain why saying "the average salary is $85,000" can be technically true but practically misleading.
2. A news headline reads: "Average home price rises 12%." What follow-up questions would a statistically literate reader ask?
3. When would the median be a more honest measure than the mean? Give a real-world business example.
4. Why do you think people say "average" instead of "mean" in everyday speech — and does that matter?
5. If a company can legally choose which measure of central tendency to report, should there be regulations requiring them to specify which one? Defend your answer.

## Assessment Rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1) | Proficient (2) | Exemplary (3) |
|-----------|----------------|----------------|---------------|
| **Conceptual Accuracy** | Confuses mean, median, and mode; cannot distinguish them | Correctly defines mean, median, and mode; understands mean is one type of average | Clearly articulates the relationship between "mean" and "average" and explains why the distinction matters in context |
| **Spreadsheet Application** | Cannot apply formulas independently; significant errors in dataset | Correctly applies AVERAGE, MEDIAN, and MODE formulas with minor errors | Accurately computes all three measures, correctly interprets outlier effects, and documents findings clearly |
| **Critical Analysis** | Accepts data claims at face value; does not question which measure is used | Identifies that the choice of measure affects interpretation; gives a basic explanation | Evaluates the ethical and communicative implications of measure selection; connects to real business or economic decisions |
| **Communication** | Responses are vague or off-topic during discussion and journaling | Responses show understanding and use correct vocabulary (mean, median, outlier, central tendency) | Responses are precise, well-reasoned, and connect statistical thinking to broader social or economic contexts |
