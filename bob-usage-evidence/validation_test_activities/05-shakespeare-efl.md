# Language Arts: Shakespeare's Language Through Bilingual Lens (Portuguese/English EFL)

## Narrative Hook

Four hundred years ago, a playwright in London invented words — "swagger," "eyeball," "bedroom" — that had never been used before. Today, you'll experience Shakespeare's language in two tongues, decode his wordplay, and discover that you are thinking like he thinks: making old words do new jobs.

## Materials

- Printed or projected scene excerpt (e.g., from Romeo and Juliet, Act 2 Scene 2, first 15 lines)
- Printed side-by-side English/Portuguese translation
- Vocabulary sticky note cards (one per word, pre-written — 15 key words from the scene)
- Large paper or whiteboard space for word mapping
- Journals for reflection
- Audio recording of the scene in both languages (optional but highly recommended)

---

## Step-by-Step Instructions

1. **Phase 1 — Hook & Word Invention (5 min)**
   - Read or paraphrase the hook.
   - Ask: "Can you invent a new word right now in English? Or Portuguese?" Take 2–3 attempts.
   - Explain: Shakespeare did this so often that about 1,700 words we use today were first written down by him — bedroom, fashionable, eyeball.
   - Ask: "How did he create new words?" Guide students toward: he combined old words, used nouns as verbs, or added prefixes/suffixes.

2. **Phase 2 — Comparative Word Study (15 min)**
   - Display 5 key words from the scene in English (e.g., "thou," "wherefore," "What light through yonder window breaks").
   - Ask: "What do these mean? Try to guess from context or from the sound."
   - Show the Portuguese translations and read both aloud. Ask: "Do the words take up the same space in each language? Why might that matter for the rhythm?"
   - Explain that Shakespeare wrote in iambic pentameter — a pattern of unstressed-stressed syllables. Some words fit that pattern in English but not Portuguese (or vice versa). When translators adapt his work, they have to choose: stay faithful to meaning, or preserve the rhythm?
   - Post the comparative word list on the wall. Mark which words are "close cousins" (cognates) in both languages vs. which ones are completely different.

3. **Phase 3 — Mini-Scene Reading (20 min)**
   - Distribute the scene excerpt printed side-by-side in English and Portuguese (aligned line-by-line).
   - If available, play a brief audio recording (2–3 minutes) of an actor performing it in English. Students follow along on paper, listening for rhythm.
   - Ask: "Did the words sound different from how you speak English? Why?"
   - Explain Early Modern English pronunciation, contracted forms, and poetic license very briefly.
   - Then read the Portuguese version aloud (or play recording). Students compare the experience: "Does it feel different in Portuguese? Did you understand more or less?"
   - ✏️ [INSERT TEACHER NOTE HERE] — "Psychological safety point: This is not a comprehension test. Both languages are challenging. The goal is experiencing the strangeness of poetic language, not translating perfectly."

4. **Phase 4 — Vocabulary & Wordplay Mapping (15 min)**
   - Distribute the sticky note vocabulary cards.
   - Each student gets 2–3 cards. They find their word(s) in the printed scene and underline them.
   - On a large piece of paper at the front, draw a word web with "Shakespeare's Inventions" in the centre. Invite students to place their cards around it and say aloud: "My word is ___. In the scene, it means ___."
   - Connect related words with lines (e.g., "light" connects to "darkness," "window" connects to "sight").
   - Explain one or two examples of wordplay (if present in the chosen scene, e.g., a pun on "grave" meaning both burial and serious in Romeo and Juliet).

5. **Phase 5 — Bilingual Creative Response (25 min)**
   - Students choose one of the following creative tasks (explain all options first):
     - **Option A (Writing):** Write your own 4-line love declaration to an imaginary person, using one of Shakespeare's invented words or poetic devices. Write it in English or Portuguese, or mix them (code-switching is allowed and celebrated).
     - **Option B (Performance):** Memorise 2–3 lines from the scene in English and 2–3 in Portuguese, and practise saying them with feeling. Be ready to share with a partner or small group.
     - **Option C (Visual):** Draw or collage a scene that captures the emotional heart of the excerpt (e.g., "star-crossed lovers" or "forbidden longing"). Label it with key words from the scene in both languages.
   - Circulate and provide oral feedback without judgment. Celebrate attempts at rhythm, wordplay, and bilingual code-switching.
   - ✏️ [INSERT TEACHER NOTE HERE] — "For students anxious about EFL performance, pair Option B (memorisation) with a trusted partner. For students stronger in written expression, prioritise Options A and C."

6. **Phase 6 — Reflection & Consolidation (10 min)**
   - Invite 2–3 volunteers to share their creative work (not mandatory).
   - Return to the original hook: "Shakespeare invented words. Did you invent anything today?" Discuss how students played with language across two tongues.
   - Students respond to one reflection question (see below) in their journals.

---

## Reflection Questions

1. Was it easier or harder to understand Shakespeare in Portuguese than in English? Why do you think so?
2. "Wherefore art thou Romeo?" means "Why are you Romeo?" But most people think it means "Where are you, Romeo?" Why do you think this mistake is so common? What does that tell us about language changing over time?
3. Shakespeare invented words by breaking the rules. What new word would you invent in English or Portuguese? What would it mean, and how would you use it?
4. When you switched between English and Portuguese today, did anything feel lost in translation? What?
5. If you were translating Romeo and Juliet into Portuguese, would you prioritise keeping the meaning exactly the same or keeping the rhythm? Defend your choice.

## Assessment Rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1) | Proficient (2) | Exemplary (3) |
|-----------|----------------|----------------|---------------|
| **Comprehension of Shakespearean language** | Cannot access the meaning even with translation support | Understands the gist of the scene with support (translation, teacher prompts) | Identifies the emotional core and specific wordplay; connects to deeper themes |
| **Bilingual awareness** | Uses only one language throughout; misses comparative opportunities | Uses both languages but treats them as separate systems | Recognises cognates, explores code-switching, and articulates how each language shapes meaning differently |
| **Creative language use** | Creative response is minimal or copied from the model | Creative response uses vocabulary from the lesson and shows some playfulness with language | Creative response demonstrates original thinking, playful language experimentation, and confidence across both languages |
| **Reflection depth** | Responses are yes/no or one sentence | Responses show engagement with the question and some reasoning | Responses are thoughtful, use examples from the lesson, and reveal personal linguistic insight |
