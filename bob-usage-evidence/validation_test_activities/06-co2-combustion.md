# Chemistry: CO₂ Combustion — Breaking the Circular Reasoning Loop

## Narrative Hook

Strike a match. It burns. Blow it out. The smoke disappears. Where did it go? A student says, "The carbon burned, so it became CO₂." The teacher asks, "How do you know?" The student says, "Because when things burn, they make CO₂." But that's circular — and today we're going to see why one answer makes the loop.

## Materials

- Pre-lit candle (teacher demonstation only; high school lab with Bunsen burner if upper secondary)
- Clear glass jar or beaker to cover flame (for suffocation demo)
- Limewater (calcium hydroxide solution) — 2 test tubes + dropper
- Drinking straws
- White paper or white card (background for observations)
- Safety goggles for any student near combustion
- Journals or worksheet paper
- Whiteboard for reasoning map

---

## Step-by-Step Instructions

1. **Phase 1 — Hook & Circular Reasoning Identification (10 min)**
   - Ask the class the hook question: "When something burns, what happens to it?"
   - Take 3–4 responses. Write each one on the board.
   - Identify a circular explanation together: "Things burn → they make CO₂. How do we know? Because burned things become CO₂."
   - Ask: "Is that the same claim twice? What would we need to see — not hear — to prove it's really CO₂?"
   - Guide toward the idea of independent evidence (not just assuming).

2. **Phase 2 — Combustion Observation (10 min)**
   - TEACHER DEMO (not student-led at primary level): Light a candle in a well-ventilated space or lab hood. Hold it in a clear jar or let it burn in the open.
   - Ask students to describe exactly what they see, not what they think is happening: "What colour is the flame? What shape? What leaves the candle and goes into the air? Is it blue, white, invisible?"
   - Now cover the flame with a clear glass jar. Ask: "Why did the flame go out? What was used up?" (Guide toward oxygen, the air.)
   - Light it again. Hold white paper nearby: "Do you see anything landing on the paper?" (Soot — carbon that didn't fully combust.)
   - Remove the paper and ask: "If the carbon burned completely, would there be soot?" (Lead them toward: complete burning = no solid left, no soot, invisible products.)

3. **Phase 3 — Limewater Test (20 min)**
   - Explain: "Limewater is clear. If CO₂ is present, limewater turns white (cloudy)." This is a diagnostic test — if we see the change, CO₂ was there.
   - Pour limewater into two test tubes. Keep one as a control (not exposed to anything). Have students take turns breathing through a straw into the second tube for 30 seconds.
   - Observation: the tube with breath becomes cloudy. Ask: "What does that tell us?" (We exhale CO₂.)
   - Now have students repeat with a small safe-demo or a lit candle smoke carefully piped in (teacher-controlled): the limewater turns cloudy.
   - Ask: "What just happened?" (CO₂ from the candle turned the limewater cloudy, just like our breath did.)

4. **Phase 4 — Analysis & Reasoning Mapping (10 min)**
   - On the whiteboard, draw two boxes:
     - Left box: "The circular claim: Things burn → they make CO₂ → therefore things burn ✓"
     - Right box: "Breaking the circle: (1) We see flame, heat, soot. (2) We don't see CO₂ (it's invisible). (3) We test with limewater → colour change. (4) Conclusion: CO₂ was present."
   - Highlight the difference: The second path uses independent evidence (limewater), not re-stating the assumption.
   - Ask: "Which path is a real explanation?" (The second one — it has evidence.)

5. **Phase 5 — Debrief & Reflection (10 min)**
   - Ask: "In the original circular reasoning, where did it go wrong? The logic seemed fine — why wasn't it enough?"
   - Guide toward: Assuming the answer doesn't prove the answer. You need evidence you can test or observe independently.
   - Students complete their reflection (see below).

---

## Reflection Questions

1. Before the limewater test, why did you believe things that burn make CO₂? Was that a good enough reason?
2. What did the limewater test tell us that just watching the candle couldn't tell us?
3. If a student said, "Lightning makes noise because it's a loud explosion," is that circular reasoning? Why or why not?
4. Give an example of a circular explanation you've heard (maybe not in science — could be anywhere). How would you test it?
5. Why do scientists use tests like limewater instead of just thinking through the logic?

## Assessment Rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1) | Proficient (2) | Advanced (3) |
|-----------|----------------|----------------|-------------|
| **Identifies circular reasoning** | Cannot articulate the circular logic even with prompting | Recognises that "things burn make CO₂ because burned things are CO₂" restates itself but may not fully explain why it's a problem | Clearly explains that circular reasoning restates the claim without providing independent evidence |
| **Interprets the limewater evidence** | Sees the colour change but doesn't connect it to the presence of CO₂ | Correctly interprets the limewater test as evidence for CO₂ presence | Explains why limewater is an independent test, distinguishing it from simple observation or assumption |
| **Applies the reasoning structure** | Cannot use the reasoning map or create one independently | Can place evidence and reasoning into the provided framework with teacher scaffolding | Independently constructs a reasoning chain from observation → test → evidence → conclusion |
| **Reflection quality** | Response is vague or shows incomplete understanding | Response engages the question and shows that the student followed the lab reasoning | Response articulates the difference between assumption and evidence, with a clear personal example |
