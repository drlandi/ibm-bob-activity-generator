# Social Studies: Water Scarcity in Botswana — A Global Challenge with Local Faces

## Narrative Hook

Botswana is one of Africa's wealthiest countries — strong economy, stable government, world-class schools. But 40% of its territory is the Kalahari Desert. Rain falls unpredictably. Aquifers are being drained faster than they refill. So what happens when money can't buy what nature is running out of? Today, you'll meet a village and follow the choices they face.

## Materials

- Projected map of southern Africa showing Botswana, the Kalahari, and major water sources
- Printed case study brief (one per group of 3–4 students) — 2–3 pages, age-appropriate narrative of a fictional village facing water scarcity
- Chart paper and markers (one per group)
- Student journals or reflection sheets
- Optional: a single picture or photograph of a Botswana landscape (Kalahari, well, or water point)

---

## Step-by-Step Instructions

1. **Phase 1 — Hook Story & Map Orientation (10 min)**
   - Read or paraphrase the hook aloud.
   - Display a map of southern Africa. Point to Botswana. Show the Kalahari Desert region and major water sources (Okavango Delta, Limpopo River).
   - Ask: "If your country is rich but water is scarce, which problem is harder to solve — lack of money or lack of water?" Collect 2–3 responses without evaluating.
   - Explain that today they'll investigate real choices people in Botswana face.

2. **Phase 2 — Village Case Study (15 min)**
   - Divide the class into groups of 3–4. Give each group a printed case study (see Customisation Zones for suggested story content).
   - Sample scenario: "The village of Serowe has 65,000 people. The groundwater aquifer is dropping 1 metre per year. The nearest alternative water source — the Makgadigadi Pans — is 100 km away and extremely salty. The government has proposed drilling deeper wells. Local farmers say this will destroy the grassland for cattle. What should the village do?"
   - Groups read the case together (aloud, one student per paragraph, or silently then discuss key facts). Teacher circulates to check comprehension.
   - Each group appoints a spokesperson and a scribe.

3. **Phase 3 — Scarcity Challenge Mapping (15 min)**
   - Each group draws a simple diagram on chart paper with three sections:
     - "The problem" (what is happening to water)
     - "Who it affects" (people, animals, farms, businesses)
     - "Possible choices" (one or more options, even if imperfect)
   - Groups fill in each section based on their case study. Encourage specificity: "affected" should name teachers, farmers, children, animals, etc.
   - The "possible choices" section should list 2–3 options from the case (even if ones characters disagree on).

4. **Phase 4 — Group Discussion & Solutions (10 min)**
   - Each group spends 5 minutes debating: "What should we choose, and why?" Recorder writes the group's recommendation (e.g., "Drill deeper wells but also enforce agricultural water limits").
   - Bring the class back together. Each spokesperson presents their problem map and recommendation in one minute. No cross-talk yet.

5. **Phase 5 — Reflection & Whole-Class Synthesis (10 min)**
   - Display all four (or however many groups) recommendations on the board side by side.
   - Ask: "Why did different groups choose different answers if they all read the same case?" (Different values, different assumptions about consequences, different willingness to accept risk.)
   - Explain that real-world policy disagreement often isn't about facts — it's about which consequences matter most and who bears the cost.
   - Students respond to reflection questions in their journals (see below).

---

## Reflection Questions

1. Did you find it easy or hard to choose what Serowe should do? What made the choice difficult?
2. Who in the village would prefer drilling deeper? Who might oppose it? Explain.
3. If you were the village leader and people disagreed on the solution, what would you do?
4. In your home region or country, what resource (water, land, minerals, forests) is running short? How is it similar to or different from Botswana's water crisis?
5. Is it fair that some countries have abundant water while others don't? What could the world community do about that?

## Assessment Rubric

| Criterion | Approaching (1) | Proficient (2) | Exemplary (3) |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|---------------|
| **Case study comprehension** | Student recalls 1 or fewer facts from the reading | Student recalls 2–3 key facts (the problem, one affected group, one proposed solution) | Student accurately summarises the case and articulates the central conflict or dilemma |
| **Systems thinking** | Identifies only one aspect of the problem (e.g., drought) | Identifies the problem and names two groups affected or two consequences | Explains how the problem affects multiple groups/systems and how one solution might create new problems |
| **Decision-making reasoning** | Recommends a choice but gives little or no reason | Recommends a choice with one clear reason (e.g., "because the land is important") | Recommends a choice, anticipates one consequence or trade-off, and explains the value judgment underneath the choice |
| **Reflection depth** | Response is surface-level or off-topic | Response shows understanding of the case and attempts to connect to personal experience | Response is thoughtful, connects local to global, and reflects genuine grappling with the tension between needs |

## Customization Zones

**Localise the case study** — replace Botswana with a water-scarce region in your own country or one locally relevant to your students. The structure (rich country, water-poor region, conflicting solutions) stays the same but the details become real to their world.

**Extend into personal water audit** — after the case study discussion, have students track their own water use for one day (showers, brushing teeth, washing hands, flushing toilets, watering plants) and estimate litres. Compare individual totals with global per-capita averages. Connect back to Serowe: "If everyone in our class drank like Serowe, how long would the aquifer last?"

**Role-play version** — instead of group discussion, assign roles within each group: village farmer, water company executive, environmental activist, government representative. Each must argue their position based on the case study. After 5 minutes, de-assign roles and ask: "How did wearing that role change how you saw the problem?"
✏️ [INSERT TEACHER NOTE HERE] — Role plays can feel forced in some classrooms; offer the option, don't require it.

**Global water mapping** — display a world map showing water scarcity regions (Africa, Middle East, western North America, parts of Asia). Have students mark Botswana and find one other scarcity region to research briefly. Compare: how are they similar? Different?
