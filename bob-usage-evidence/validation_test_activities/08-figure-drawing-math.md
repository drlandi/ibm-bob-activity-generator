# Visual Arts & Mathematics: Proportion, Pattern, and Measurement in Figure Drawing

## Narrative Hook

Stand in front of a mirror and measure your face. Eye to nose: one nose-width. Nose to chin: one nose-width. Top of head to eyebrows: one and a half nose-widths. Draw those proportions onto a blank face and suddenly it looks alive — not like a badly-drawn egg. Today, you'll use measurement to unlock the art of figure drawing.

## Materials

- Mirrors (one per pair or small group)
- Rulers (one per student)
- Pencils (regular and eraser)
- Paper (mixed sizes: 8.5"×11" and larger optional)
- Pre-drawn proportional grid diagram (poster size, for display)
- Optional: printed reference sheet showing proportional rules (face, full body)
- Journals or reflection sheets

---

## Step-by-Step Instructions

1. **Phase 1 — Hook & Self-Measurement (10 min)**
   - Read or paraphrase the hook.
   - Give each pair of students one mirror. Ask them to measure their own faces using a ruler (or a piece of string, then measure the string): eye to nose, nose to chin, etc.
   - Take 3–4 students' measurements and write them on the board. Ask: "Are they all exactly the same? Close? Why do you think?"
   - Introduce the idea of proportional rules: artists use a unit (like "one nose-width") to keep the relationship between features consistent, even though faces vary.

2. **Phase 2 — Learn Proportional Rules (10 min)**
   - Display the proportional grid diagram (or draw it on the board). Introduce the basic rules:
     - **Face proportions:** The head is about 7–8 "head-widths" tall (using head-width as the unit)
     - **Eyes:** Placed roughly 1/3 down from the top of the head
     - **Nose:** Ends about halfway down the face
     - **Mouth:** About 2/3 down
     - Ears align with eye to nose line
     - Distance between eyes is about one eye-width
   - Draw a simple face diagram together, measuring as you go. Do not make it perfect — the goal is showing the process, not creating a masterpiece.
   - Ask: "If you know these units, can you draw a face that looks alive?" (Yes, because the proportions will feel right even if your line quality isn't perfect.)

3. **Phase 3 — Guided Practice Face Drawing (15 min)**
   - Each student draws a face on their paper using the proportional rules. Provide step-by-step instruction:
     - Step 1: Lightly draw an oval for the head shape with a ruler (measure roughly — head-height = 5 inches, for example).
     - Step 2: Divide the head in half horizontally (eye line).
     - Step 3: Divide the lower half in half again (nose line) and once more (mouth line).
     - Step 4: Place eyes on the eye line, one eye-width apart.
     - Step 5: Add nose, mouth, ears, hair using the guide lines.
   - Circulate and provide individual feedback: "Your proportions look good — now let me see your next one from scratch."
   - Emphasise: "Your ruler is your friend. If it looks wrong, measure again."

4. **Phase 4 — Independent Full-Figure Sketch with Ruler (15 min)**
   - Explain that the whole body also follows proportional rules. Provide the basic guide:
     - Head = 1 unit
     - Shoulders to waist = 1 unit
     - Waist to knees = 1 unit
     - Knees to feet = 1 unit
     - Total body height ≈ 7–8 head units
   - Students attempt a full-figure sketch (standing figure) using their ruler and these proportions. They do not need to add detailed features — simple stick-like or geometric shapes are fine for this exercise.
   - Optional: Provide a reference figure (printed photo of a person or an artist's proportional diagram).
   - ✏️ [INSERT TEACHER NOTE HERE] — "Emphasise that this is about understanding proportion, not creating gallery-quality art. Awkward poses and stiff drawings are part of learning."

5. **Phase 5 — Peer Feedback & Refinement (10 min)**
   - Pair students. Each shows their figure to a partner and the partner checks:
     - Are the head, shoulders, waist, and legs roughly equal in height?
     - Are the eyes spaced correctly on the face?
     - Is the neck visible and reasonable in thickness?
   - Partner gives one piece of positive feedback and asks one clarifying question (e.g., "Is that arm supposed to be longer?").
   - If time and energy allow, students make one small refinement based on feedback.

6. **Phase 6 — Reflection & Share-Out (10 min)**
   - Display 5–6 student drawings on the board or on a wall gallery walk (optional).
   - Ask the class: "What do you notice about the ones that feel most alive or most 'real'?" (Guide toward: good proportions, even if the drawing is simple.)
   - Each student completes a brief reflection (see below).

---

## Reflection Questions

1. Before this activity, did you think you "couldn't draw"? Has your thinking changed? Why?
2. When you used the ruler and proportions, did your figure look better or worse than when you drew without measuring? Explain.
3. Why do you think artists learn these proportional rules even though real people don't all have the same proportions?
4. If you keep practising, you'll eventually draw faces and figures without a ruler and they'll still look right. Why do you think that happens?
5. Did any step feel easy? Difficult? What would help you improve?

## Assessment Rubric

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|----------------|
| **Understanding of proportional rules** | Student cannot articulate or apply any proportional guideline | Student understands 1–2 rules (e.g., eye placement, head-width as a unit) but applies inconsistently | Student consistently applies proportional rules to both face and figure; explains the reasoning |
| **Use of measurement tools** | Student does not use ruler; measurements are haphazard | Student uses ruler occasionally but not systematically | Student uses ruler to verify proportions and adjusts as needed |
| **Quality of final drawings** | Drawings lack recognisable facial features or body proportions; seem random | Drawings show some intentional proportional structure but with gaps or inconsistencies | Drawings clearly demonstrate proportional understanding; face and figure both show accurate relative sizing |
| **Reflection & learning attitude** | Reflection is absent or dismissive of the learning ("I can't draw") | Reflection shows some growth mindset; student acknowledges the connection between measurement and improved drawings | Reflection is thoughtful and specific; student recognises the role of skill-building and proportion rules in art-making |

## Customization Zones

**Swap the figure type** — instead of a standing figure, students draw a face in profile, a seated figure, or a hand. Each has its own proportional rules that can be taught the same way.

**Make it more art-focused** — after the proportional drawing phase, students add shading, background, clothing, or emotion to their figure. This keeps the proportional learning but lets artistic expression flow.

**Make it more mathematical** — extend into a unit on ratio and scaling. "If your head is 2 inches tall and your full figure is 7 heads, how tall is the figure? If you want to scale it up to 14 inches tall, what's the new head size?" Connect drawing to actual algebra.

**Measurement variation gallery** — display the class's proportional measurements (from mirrors) and compare. Create a chart: "Our head-to-nose ratios ranged from ___ to ___. Why?" Celebrate diversity while explaining that the proportional *system* works across human variation.
