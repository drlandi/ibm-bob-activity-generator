# Interdisciplinary: Food Systems Design — Resilience, Ethics, and Local Constraints

## Narrative Hook

Your city produces almost no food. Every apple, every grain of rice arrives by truck, ship, or plane — sometimes from 10,000 km away. Then a storm hits the highways. Trucks stop. Supermarkets run empty. What happens to 2 million people in 48 hours? Today, you'll design a food system that doesn't break when the delivery trucks don't arrive.

## Materials

- Large paper (newsprint or flip-chart size) for each group
- Markers, coloured pencils, or pastels
- Research handouts (1 per group): profiles of local agriculture, current supply chains, climate data, land availability, community resources
- Constraint cards (printed cards listing real-world limitations)
- Sticky notes for feedback
- Journals or reflection sheets

---

## Step-by-Step Instructions

1. **Phase 1 — Hook & Systems Mapping (10 min)**
   - Read the hook aloud. Show a map of your city and the surrounding region (or a generic city for adaptability).
   - Ask: "Where does your breakfast come from? Draw the journey of a tomato from where it grows to your plate."
   - Invite 2–3 students to describe their tomato's journey. Write the steps on the board: Farm → Distributor → Truck → Warehouse → Store → Home.
   - Ask: "What could break this chain?" (Weather, fuel prices, truck breakdown, labour shortage, etc.)
   - Explain: "Today, you'll design a system that's more resilient — harder to break."

2. **Phase 2 — Introduce Constraints & Values (10 min)**
   - Divide the class into groups of 4–5 (the design teams).
   - Give each group a set of constraint cards. Sample constraints:
     - Land available for urban/peri-urban farming: 5% of current agricultural land
     - Growing season in this climate: 6 months
     - Budget for infrastructure: $500,000 per 100,000 people
     - Labour force: mix of volunteers + part-time workers
     - Storage capacity: limited (think about spoilage and rot)
   - Ask groups to pick three values they want their food system to embody: e.g., "local," "affordable," "organic," "job-creating," "nutritious," "efficient."
   - Each group records their values and constraints on a card to display.

3. **Phase 3 — Design Challenge (30–40 min per session, spans 2–3 sessions)**
   - Task: "Design a food system for a city of 2 million that can feed the population for at least 30 days if all truck deliveries stop. Your design must respect your constraints and embody your values."
   - On their large paper, groups sketch or diagram:
     - **Where food is grown** (rooftop farms? community gardens? vertical farms? peri-urban belt? mix?)
     - **What is grown** (which crops suit the climate and can be stored long-term?)
     - **How it's distributed** (local markets? community-supported agriculture [CSA]? food cooperatives? home delivery?)
     - **How it's stored** (cold storage? root cellars? preservation/canning?)
     - **Who does the work** (paid farmers? volunteers? schools? prisons [ethically]? mix?)
     - **How it's funded** (city budget? private investment? community funding?)
   - Circulate and ask probing questions: "If you run out of water, can your rooftop farms keep producing?" "Who eats if they can't afford your food?" "What happens to crop waste?"
   - ✏️ [INSERT TEACHER NOTE HERE] — "Groups may feel overwhelmed. Help them focus on one piece (e.g., 'Let's start with 100,000 people, not 2 million') and scale up."

4. **Phase 4 — Prototype Refinement (20 min, typically in session 2)**
   - Groups review their first draft. Pose new scenarios:
     - "A disease kills 40% of the crop. What's your backup?"
     - "3,000 unemployed people want to work in food production. How do you employ them sustainably?"
     - "A neighbourhood rejects a vertical farm being built near them. What do you do?"
   - Groups refine their proposal in response to one scenario of their choice.
   - 📝 [INSERT STUDENT WORKSHEET HERE] — Scenario cards (printed, one per group or distributed round-robin)

5. **Phase 5 — Peer Feedback Carousel (20 min, session 3)**
   - Post each group's design on the wall around the room.
   - Each student gets 2–3 sticky notes.
   - Students walk around and leave sticky-note feedback for each group using a frame: "I appreciate ___. I wonder ___. I notice ___."
   - Groups read their feedback and identify the most challenging question or concern.

6. **Phase 6 — Reflection & Final Revision (10 min)**
   - Groups make one final revision based on feedback (or choose not to, if they stand by their design).
   - Each group writes a one-paragraph summary of their design philosophy: "Why is this system better than the current one? What would you do differently if you had unlimited resources?"
   - Students complete their reflection (see below).

---

## Reflection Questions

1. What was the hardest constraint to work within? Why was it hard?
2. Did your group's values conflict with your constraints? Give an example and explain how you handled it.
3. Who did you design this food system for — everyone in the city equally, or were some groups prioritised? Defend your choice.
4. If this design became real, what would change most in your daily life? Would you like that change?
5. What did this design challenge teach you about interconnectedness? (Agriculture, jobs, climate, inequality, logistics — what connections surprised you?)

## Assessment Rubric

| Criterion | Approaching (1) | Proficient (2) | Exemplary (3) |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|---------------|
| **Systems thinking** | Design addresses only one aspect of food production (e.g., growing crops) | Design includes 3–4 components (growing, distributing, storing, funding) but connections are loose | Design integrates all major components (growing, distributing, storing, funding, labour, crisis response) with clear interdependencies |
| **Incorporation of constraints** | Constraints are listed but not actually reflected in the design | Design respects 2–3 constraints but may overlook or minimise others | Design actively acknowledges constraints and explains how each is managed or intentionally challenged |
| **Values alignment** | Values are stated but disconnected from design choices | Most design choices align with stated values; one or two trade-offs are unclear | Every major design choice is traced back to the group's values; trade-offs are explicit and defended |
| **Resilience & feasibility** | Design is fragile (would collapse under the given scenario) or wildly unrealistic | Design shows some resilience (addresses one or two failure modes) and is mostly realistic | Design demonstrates multiple layers of resilience; is ambitious but evidence-based (e.g., references real urban farms, CSA models) |
| **Reflection depth** | Reflection is surface-level; misses the systems insight | Reflection shows understanding of constraints and values but limited connection to broader interconnectedness | Reflection articulates deep understanding of systems interdependence, acknowledges ethical tensions, and considers personal and societal implications |

## Customization Zones

**Adjust the timeline** — this activity can be compressed into a single 90-minute session by: (a) pre-selecting constraints for groups, (b) limiting the design to one component (e.g., "just grow the food," not distribution), (c) simplifying the city population to 100,000.

**Localise to your region** — replace the generic city with your actual city or a nearby one. Use real climate data, real land availability, real supply chains. Partner with a local urban farmer or food-bank manager to provide guest input on the challenges.
